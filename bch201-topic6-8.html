Topics 6–8: Carbohydrates, Lipids and Nucleic Acids

MLS Study Hub

Learning Objectives

After studying this page, students should be able to:

  • Classify carbohydrates and explain their important properties and functions.
  • Describe the major classes of lipids and their biological roles.
  • Explain the structures and functions of DNA and RNA.
  • Differentiate nucleosides from nucleotides and understand basic nomenclature.
  • Describe the effects of acid and alkali hydrolysis on nucleic acids.
Study reminder: Learn each biomolecule by its building blocks, classification, chemical properties, functions and laboratory significance.

TOPIC 6: CARBOHYDRATES

1. Definition

Carbohydrates are biomolecules that include sugars and their polymers. Many contain carbon, hydrogen and oxygen, and simple carbohydrates often approximate the formula Cn(H2O)n. However, this formula does not apply to every carbohydrate.

2. Classification of carbohydrates

ClassDescriptionExamples
MonosaccharidesSingle sugar units that cannot be hydrolysed into smaller carbohydrates.Glucose, fructose, ribose
DisaccharidesTwo monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond.Sucrose, lactose, maltose
OligosaccharidesShort chains containing a few monosaccharide units.Raffinose and carbohydrate chains in glycoproteins
PolysaccharidesLong chains of monosaccharide units.Starch, glycogen, cellulose

3. Monosaccharides by functional group

Aldoses contain an aldehyde group in their open-chain form, while ketoses contain a ketone group. Glucose is an example of an aldose; fructose is an example of a ketose.

Monosaccharides can also be classified by carbon number: trioses have three carbons, pentoses have five and hexoses have six.

Examples by carbon number
Triose: glyceraldehyde Pentose: ribose Hexose: glucose

4. Important monosaccharides

  • Glucose: a major fuel for many cells and an important blood-glucose measurement in clinical laboratories.
  • Fructose: a sugar found in fruits and honey and a component of sucrose.
  • Galactose: a component of lactose and certain complex carbohydrates.
  • Ribose: a sugar found in RNA and several biologically important molecules.
  • Deoxyribose: the sugar component of DNA nucleotides.

5. Disaccharides and their components

DisaccharideComponentsReducing property
MaltoseGlucose + glucoseReducing sugar
LactoseGalactose + glucoseReducing sugar
SucroseGlucose + fructoseNon-reducing sugar in common tests

A glycosidic bond links sugar units. Hydrolysis breaks this bond by using water, releasing smaller sugar units.

6. Polysaccharides

PolysaccharideMain roleWhere it is found
StarchEnergy storagePlants
GlycogenEnergy storageAnimals, especially liver and muscle
CelluloseStructural supportPlant cell walls
ChitinStructural supportArthropod exoskeletons and fungal cell walls

7. Important chemical properties

  • Many monosaccharides dissolve readily in water because of their hydroxyl groups.
  • Reducing sugars can react with suitable reagents under the conditions of the test.
  • Carbohydrates can undergo oxidation, reduction and hydrolysis reactions.
  • Some carbohydrates form coloured complexes with specific reagents.
  • Monosaccharides may exist in open-chain and ring forms in solution.

8. Common laboratory tests for carbohydrates

MLS application: Glucose testing is important in assessing and monitoring disorders of blood-glucose regulation. Screening reactions are not definitive identification tests and may be affected by interfering substances.

TOPIC 7: LIPIDS

1. Definition of lipids

Lipids are a diverse group of biological substances that are generally poorly soluble in water and more soluble in non-polar organic solvents. They include fats, oils, phospholipids, sterols and waxes. Not all lipids have the same structure.

2. Classification of lipids

ClassDescriptionExamples
Simple lipidsEsters of fatty acids with alcohols.Triacylglycerols and waxes
Compound or complex lipidsContain additional groups such as phosphate or carbohydrate.Phospholipids and glycolipids
Derived lipidsSubstances obtained from lipid breakdown or related lipid compounds.Fatty acids, glycerol, steroid compounds

3. Fatty acids

Fatty acids contain a hydrocarbon chain and a terminal carboxyl group. They are important components of many lipids.

  • Saturated fatty acids: contain no carbon-carbon double bonds in the hydrocarbon chain.
  • Unsaturated fatty acids: contain one or more carbon-carbon double bonds.
  • Monounsaturated: contain one double bond.
  • Polyunsaturated: contain two or more double bonds.

Chain length and degree of unsaturation influence the physical properties of fats. The arrangement of double bonds can also affect melting behaviour.

4. Essential fatty acids

Essential fatty acids must be obtained from the diet because the human body cannot make sufficient amounts of them. Linoleic acid (omega-6) and alpha-linolenic acid (omega-3) are essential fatty acids.

They contribute to cell membrane composition and are precursors of signalling molecules. Other omega-3 fatty acids, including EPA and DHA, have important biological roles and may be obtained from dietary sources or formed to varying degrees from alpha-linolenic acid.

5. Triacylglycerols

Triacylglycerols, commonly called triglycerides, consist of one glycerol molecule esterified with three fatty acids. They are a major form of energy storage in humans and many other organisms.

General structure of a triacylglycerol
Glycerol backbone+ 3 fatty acids→ Triacylglycerol + water during ester formation

Three ester bonds link the fatty acids to glycerol.

6. Phospholipids

Phospholipids contain a phosphate-containing polar region and hydrophobic portions. Many membrane phospholipids have two fatty-acid tails and form bilayers in water.

○ represents polar heads; ║ represents hydrophobic tails. The tails face inward, away from water.

Phospholipids are major components of cell membranes and help create a selective barrier between a cell and its environment.

7. Steroids and cholesterol

Steroids contain a characteristic structure of four fused rings. Cholesterol is an important component of animal cell membranes and is a precursor for steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D.

Cholesterol is transported in blood by lipoproteins. LDL and HDL are particles with different compositions and functions; their clinical interpretation depends on the full lipid profile and overall risk factors.

8. Other important lipids

  • Waxes: provide protective, water-resistant coatings in plants and animals.
  • Glycolipids: contain carbohydrate groups and contribute to membrane structure and cell recognition.
  • Eicosanoids: signalling molecules derived from certain polyunsaturated fatty acids; they participate in processes such as inflammation.
  • Lipoproteins: transport triglycerides, cholesterol and other lipids through blood and other body fluids.

9. Biological functions of lipids

FunctionExplanation
Energy storageTriacylglycerols provide a concentrated energy reserve.
Membrane structurePhospholipids, cholesterol and glycolipids contribute to cell membranes.
Thermal insulationAdipose tissue helps reduce heat loss.
ProtectionFat around some organs provides cushioning.
SignallingSteroid hormones and eicosanoids regulate biological processes.
Vitamin absorptionLipids support absorption of fat-soluble vitamins A, D, E and K.

10. Lipid properties and reactions

  • Hydrolysis: breaks certain lipid bonds, such as ester bonds, with water.
  • Saponification: alkaline hydrolysis of suitable fats or oils produces glycerol and fatty-acid salts (soap).
  • Hydrogenation: adds hydrogen to carbon-carbon double bonds under suitable conditions.
  • Oxidation: unsaturated lipids may undergo oxidation, contributing to rancidity.
  • Emulsification: disperses fat into small droplets; bile salts help emulsify dietary fat during digestion.

11. Laboratory and clinical significance

A lipid profile commonly includes measurements such as total cholesterol, triglycerides, HDL cholesterol and calculated or measured LDL cholesterol. These results help assess cardiovascular risk when interpreted alongside age-appropriate clinical guidance and other risk factors.

Laboratory methods may include enzymatic assays and other validated analytical techniques. Specimen requirements, fasting instructions and reference intervals depend on the test and local laboratory protocol.

MLS reminder: Check the laboratory's specimen instructions, quality-control results and reporting guidelines before interpreting a lipid profile.

TOPIC 8: NUCLEIC ACIDS

1. Definition and functions

Nucleic acids are biological macromolecules made of nucleotide units. The two major types are deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA). They are essential for storing, transmitting and using genetic information.

  • DNA: stores hereditary information in most organisms.
  • RNA: functions in gene expression, protein synthesis and several regulatory and catalytic processes.

2. Components of a nucleotide

A nucleotide contains three components: a nitrogenous base, a pentose sugar and one or more phosphate groups.

Basic nucleotide structure
Nitrogenous base+ Pentose sugar+ Phosphate group(s)→ Nucleotide

The pentose sugar is ribose in RNA and 2-deoxyribose in DNA. The base attaches to the sugar, and phosphate is attached through a phosphoester linkage at a suitable sugar position.

3. Nitrogenous bases

Base groupBasesFound in
PurinesAdenine (A), guanine (G)DNA and RNA
PyrimidinesCytosine (C), thymine (T), uracil (U)Cytosine occurs in DNA and RNA; thymine mainly in DNA; uracil mainly in RNA

Purines have two fused rings; pyrimidines have one ring. DNA uses A, G, C and T, whereas RNA generally uses A, G, C and U.

4. Difference between nucleosides and nucleotides

FeatureNucleosideNucleotide
ComponentsNitrogenous base + pentose sugarNucleoside + one or more phosphate groups
ExampleAdenosine, guanosine, cytidineAMP, ADP, ATP, GMP
Phosphate present?No phosphate group in the basic nucleoside structureYes
Easy memory aid: Nucleoside = base + sugar. Nucleotide = base + sugar + phosphate.

5. Nomenclature of nucleosides

Common ribonucleoside names include adenosine, guanosine, cytidine and uridine. The corresponding deoxyribonucleosides are commonly named deoxyadenosine, deoxyguanosine, deoxycytidine and thymidine.

In a nucleoside, the base is attached to the 1′ carbon of the pentose sugar through a beta-N-glycosidic bond. Purines attach through N9, while pyrimidines attach through N1.

6. Nomenclature of nucleotides

Nucleotide names commonly indicate the nucleoside and the number or position of phosphate groups.

GMPGuanosine monophosphateGuanosine with one phosphate group

AMP, ADP and ATP are important in energy transfer and cellular metabolism. Nucleotides also form the building blocks of nucleic acids and contribute to signalling and enzyme-cofactor functions.

7. Structure of DNA

DNA usually consists of two antiparallel polynucleotide strands arranged in a double helix. The sugar-phosphate backbones lie on the outside, while the bases pair on the inside.

Complementary DNA base pairing
A—T
G≡C

Adenine pairs with thymine; guanine pairs with cytosine in standard DNA.

Nucleotides within a strand are connected by 3′–5′ phosphodiester bonds. Complementary base pairing helps DNA replicate accurately and supports the storage of genetic information.

8. Structure and types of RNA

RNA contains ribose sugar and generally uses uracil instead of thymine. Many RNA molecules are single-stranded, but they can fold into complex shapes through internal base pairing.

  • Messenger RNA (mRNA): carries coding information used in protein synthesis.
  • Transfer RNA (tRNA): delivers amino acids to the ribosome during translation.
  • Ribosomal RNA (rRNA): forms a structural and catalytic part of ribosomes.
  • Regulatory RNAs: help control gene expression and other cellular processes.

9. DNA compared with RNA

10. Hydrolysis of nucleic acids

Hydrolysis is a chemical reaction in which water helps break a chemical bond. Nucleic acids can be hydrolysed under suitable acidic, alkaline or enzymatic conditions. The products depend on the conditions and the bonds broken.

11. Acid hydrolysis

Acidic conditions can promote cleavage of bonds in nucleic acids. Depending on the acid strength, temperature and duration, hydrolysis may release bases, sugars, phosphate-containing products or smaller nucleotide fragments. Strong conditions can cause multiple reactions and damage the original structure.

  • Some glycosidic bonds between a base and sugar can be cleaved under acidic conditions.
  • Phosphodiester bonds may also be broken under sufficiently strong conditions.
  • The exact products depend on the nucleic acid and experimental conditions.

12. Alkaline hydrolysis

RNA is particularly susceptible to alkaline hydrolysis because its ribose contains a 2′-hydroxyl group. Under alkaline conditions, this group can participate in cleavage of the phosphodiester backbone, producing shorter fragments and ultimately smaller products under suitable conditions.

DNA lacks the 2′-hydroxyl group, so it is much more resistant to this particular alkaline-cleavage mechanism. This does not mean DNA is completely unaffected by every alkaline condition: strong alkali can denature double-stranded DNA by disrupting base pairing and can cause other changes depending on conditions.

13. Biological and laboratory importance

  • DNA analysis is used in genetic testing, identity investigations and some infectious-disease investigations.
  • RNA analysis supports gene-expression studies and detection of certain RNA viruses.
  • Nucleotide metabolism is important for energy transfer, cell signalling and synthesis of genetic material.
  • Appropriate specimen handling is important because nucleases can degrade nucleic acids.
  • Validated extraction and analytical procedures help protect the quality of nucleic-acid results.
MLS reminder: Use the appropriate collection tubes, transport conditions, extraction method and contamination-control procedures for the particular DNA or RNA test.

Suggested Answers

  1. Monosaccharides (glucose), disaccharides (sucrose) and polysaccharides (starch); oligosaccharides are also recognised as a class.
  2. Reducing sugars can react with suitable oxidising reagents under test conditions; glucose, lactose and maltose are examples. Sucrose is commonly classified as non-reducing.
  3. Molisch is a general carbohydrate screening test; Benedict's detects reducing substances; iodine gives a blue-black colour with starch.
  4. Lipids are a diverse group of substances generally poorly soluble in water. Classes include simple, complex and derived lipids.
  5. Saturated fatty acids have no carbon-carbon double bonds; unsaturated fatty acids have one or more.
  6. Energy storage, membrane formation, insulation, protection, signalling and absorption of fat-soluble vitamins are examples.
  7. A nitrogenous base, pentose sugar and one or more phosphate groups.
  8. A nucleoside contains a base and sugar; a nucleotide contains a base, sugar and phosphate group(s).
  9. DNA contains deoxyribose and usually thymine and is commonly double-stranded; RNA contains ribose and usually uracil and is often single-stranded.
  10. RNA has a 2′-OH group that can participate in cleavage of the phosphodiester backbone under alkaline conditions; DNA lacks this group.
  11. Acid can cleave glycosidic and, under suitable conditions, phosphodiester bonds. Products depend on the conditions.
  12. mRNA carries coding information, tRNA delivers amino acids, and rRNA forms a structural and catalytic part of ribosomes.

Topic Summary

  • Carbohydrates include sugars and polysaccharides with important energy, structural and recognition roles.
  • Lipids include fats, phospholipids, sterols and other compounds involved in energy storage, membranes and signalling.
  • Nucleosides contain a base and sugar; nucleotides additionally contain phosphate.
  • DNA stores genetic information, while RNA supports gene expression and other cellular processes.
  • Acid and alkaline hydrolysis affect nucleic acids differently; RNA is especially susceptible to alkaline backbone cleavage.
