2. Introduction to Peptides

A peptide is a compound formed when two or more amino acids are joined by peptide bonds. Amino acids are the building blocks used to construct peptides and proteins.

When the carboxyl group (-COOH) of one amino acid reacts with the amino group (-NH₂) of another amino acid, a bond forms between the carbon atom of the first group and the nitrogen atom of the second group.

In the simplified chemical equation, the reaction releases a molecule of water. This is called a condensation reaction.

Key point: A peptide bond is the covalent bond linking amino acid residues in a peptide or protein.

3. Formation of a Peptide Bond

Consider two amino acids represented as H₂N–CH(R)–COOH and H₂N–CH(R′)–COOH. The R groups represent their different side chains.

Note: This is a simplified illustration of the overall reaction.

Steps involved

  1. The carboxyl group of the first amino acid participates in the reaction.
  2. The amino group of the second amino acid participates in the reaction.
  3. A carbon–nitrogen bond forms, linking the two amino acid residues.
  4. In the simplified condensation equation, water is released.

Important biochemical note: In living cells, ribosomes form peptide bonds during protein synthesis using activated aminoacyl-tRNAs. The process is not simply the direct removal of water from free amino acids.

There is no universally fixed amino acid count separating every peptide from every protein. Length and biological structure both matter.

5. The Peptide Bond

The peptide bond is the –CO–NH– linkage between adjacent amino acid residues.

Important properties

  • Covalent: It is a strong chemical bond linking the residues.
  • Partial double-bond character: Electron delocalisation gives the C–N bond partial double-bond character.
  • Planarity: The atoms around the peptide bond generally lie in one plane.
  • Restricted rotation: Rotation around the peptide C–N bond is limited.
  • Usually trans: Most peptide bonds occur in the trans configuration; bonds involving proline can more readily adopt either configuration.
  • Polar: The carbonyl oxygen and amide nitrogen contribute to hydrogen bonding in proteins.

These properties help determine how a polypeptide chain folds into its three-dimensional structure.

6. Direction of a Peptide Chain

A peptide sequence is conventionally written from the amino terminus (N-terminus) to the carboxyl terminus (C-terminus).

The N-terminus has a free amino group, while the C-terminus has a free carboxyl group, unless modified. The internal amino and carboxyl groups are involved in peptide bonds.

7. Biological Importance of Peptides

  • Hormonal regulation: Insulin is a peptide hormone that helps regulate blood glucose.
  • Antioxidant defence: Glutathione helps protect cells against oxidative damage.
  • Nerve signalling: Some peptides act as neurotransmitters or neuromodulators.
  • Immune defence: Certain peptides help defend the body against microorganisms.
  • Cell communication: Peptides can act as signalling molecules between cells.
  • Protein formation: Polypeptide chains fold and may combine to form functional proteins.

8. Peptide Hydrolysis

Hydrolysis is the cleavage of a peptide bond through the addition of water. It can occur through the action of proteolytic enzymes or under suitable chemical conditions.

During complete hydrolysis, a peptide can be broken down into its constituent amino acids. Partial hydrolysis produces shorter peptides and may produce free amino acids.

Biological significance

  • Digestion breaks dietary proteins into smaller peptides and amino acids.
  • Proteases help regulate biological processes by cleaving specific proteins or peptides.
  • Laboratory analysis can use controlled hydrolysis to investigate protein composition.

9. Worked Examples

Example 1: How many peptide bonds are in a linear peptide?

A linear peptide containing n amino acid residues has n − 1 peptide bonds, provided it has no unusual cyclic structure.

Question: How many peptide bonds are in a linear pentapeptide?

Solution: 5 − 1 = 4 peptide bonds.

Example 2: Water released in simplified condensation

In the simplified formation of a linear peptide from free amino acids, each new peptide bond corresponds to one condensation step and the release of one water molecule.

Question: How many water molecules are released in the simplified equation for forming a linear tripeptide from three free amino acids?

Solution: 3 − 1 = 2 water molecules.

Note: This is a stoichiometric model of condensation, not a description of the actual ribosomal mechanism.

11. Answers to Revision Questions

  1. A peptide is a compound containing amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds.
  2. A peptide bond is the covalent –CO–NH– linkage between amino acid residues.
  3. In the simplified reaction, the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another, forming a C–N bond and releasing water.
  4. A dipeptide has two amino acid residues; a tripeptide has three.
  5. It is covalent, polar, planar, and has partial double-bond character with restricted rotation.
  6. The N-terminus is the amino end; the C-terminus is the carboxyl end.
  7. Examples include hormone regulation, antioxidant defence, and cell signalling.
  8. Hydrolysis is the cleavage of a peptide bond by the addition of water.
  9. 6 − 1 = 5 peptide bonds.
  10. 4 − 1 = 3 water molecules in the simplified condensation equation.

12. Key Terms

Amino acid: A building block of peptides and proteins.

Peptide: A chain of amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds.

Condensation: A reaction in which a small molecule, commonly water in the simplified equation, is released as a bond forms.

Hydrolysis: Bond cleavage involving the addition of water.

Peptide bond: The –CO–NH– linkage joining adjacent amino acid residues.

Polypeptide: A chain of many amino acid residues.
