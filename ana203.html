Chapter 1: Introduction to Human Anatomy

1.1 Definition of Anatomy

Anatomy is the scientific study of the structure of the human body and the relationships between its different parts. It examines body structures from large organs to tissues and microscopic cells.

1.2 Major Branches of Anatomy

  • Gross anatomy: Study of structures visible to the naked eye, such as the heart, lungs, liver and kidneys.
  • Microscopic anatomy: Study of structures that require a microscope. It includes cytology and histology.
  • Regional anatomy: Study of all structures within a particular region, such as the head, neck, thorax or abdomen.
  • Systemic anatomy: Study of the body system by system, such as the digestive or respiratory system.
  • Developmental anatomy: Study of structural changes from fertilization through growth and development.
  • Clinical anatomy: Application of anatomical knowledge to healthcare, diagnosis and clinical procedures.
  • Radiological anatomy: Study of body structures as seen through imaging techniques such as X-rays, CT and MRI.

1.3 Levels of Structural Organization

  1. Chemical level: Atoms and molecules, including water, proteins and DNA.
  2. Cellular level: Cells, the basic living units of the body.
  3. Tissue level: Groups of similar cells working together.
  4. Organ level: Two or more tissue types forming a structure with specific functions.
  5. Organ-system level: Organs working together to carry out major functions.
  6. Organism level: The complete human being.
Example: Cardiac muscle cells form cardiac muscle tissue. This tissue forms part of the heart, which works with blood vessels in the cardiovascular system.

Chapter 2: Anatomical Position and Directional Terms

2.1 Standard Anatomical Position

The standard anatomical position is the reference position used when describing body structures. A person stands upright, faces forward, keeps the arms at the sides, turns the palms forward, and points the feet forward.

2.2 Important Directional Terms

2.3 Anatomical Planes

  • Sagittal plane: Divides the body into right and left portions.
  • Midsagittal plane: Divides the body into equal right and left halves.
  • Coronal or frontal plane: Divides the body into anterior and posterior portions.
  • Transverse plane: Divides the body into superior and inferior portions.
  • Oblique plane: Passes through the body at an angle.

2.4 Anatomical Movements

  • Flexion: Usually decreases the angle at a joint.
  • Extension: Usually increases the angle at a joint.
  • Abduction: Moves a body part away from the midline.
  • Adduction: Moves a body part toward the midline.
  • Rotation: Turns a bone or body part around its axis.
  • Circumduction: Moves a limb in a circular, cone-like pattern.
  • Pronation: Turns the forearm so the palm faces downward in anatomical context.
  • Supination: Turns the forearm so the palm faces upward in anatomical context.

Chapter 3: Cells and Basic Tissues

3.1 The Cell

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life. Human cells differ in shape and size according to their functions.

3.2 Main Cell Structures

  • Cell membrane: Regulates movement of substances into and out of the cell.
  • Cytoplasm: Contains the cytosol and cellular structures where many reactions occur.
  • Nucleus: Contains most of the cell's DNA and helps regulate cellular activity.
  • Mitochondria: Produce much of the cell's usable energy through aerobic respiration.
  • Ribosomes: Sites of protein synthesis.
  • Rough endoplasmic reticulum: Helps produce and process proteins destined for membranes or secretion.
  • Smooth endoplasmic reticulum: Involved in lipid synthesis and other functions.
  • Golgi apparatus: Modifies, sorts and packages proteins and lipids.
  • Lysosomes: Contain enzymes for intracellular digestion.

3.3 The Four Basic Tissue Types

Epithelial tissue: Covers surfaces, lines cavities and forms glands. It protects, absorbs and secretes.

Connective tissue: Supports, binds and protects structures. Examples include bone, cartilage, blood and adipose tissue.

Muscle tissue: Produces movement. It includes skeletal, cardiac and smooth muscle.

Nervous tissue: Receives and transmits signals. It contains neurons and supporting glial cells.

MLS connection: Histology involves examining tissues microscopically, while cytology focuses on cells. Both depend on knowledge of normal cell and tissue structure.

Chapter 4: The Skeletal and Muscular Systems

4.1 The Skeletal System

The adult human skeleton is commonly described as having 206 bones, although natural anatomical variation exists. The skeleton provides support, protects organs, assists movement, stores minerals and houses bone marrow involved in blood-cell formation.

4.2 Divisions of the Skeleton

  • Axial skeleton: Skull, vertebral column, ribs and sternum.
  • Appendicular skeleton: Upper and lower limbs, shoulder girdle and pelvic girdle.

4.3 Classification of Bones

  • Long bones: Femur and humerus.
  • Short bones: Many carpal and tarsal bones.
  • Flat bones: Sternum and many skull bones.
  • Irregular bones: Vertebrae.
  • Sesamoid bones: Patella.

4.4 Joints

A joint is a location where two or more bones meet. Joints may be classified structurally as fibrous, cartilaginous or synovial. Synovial joints permit a wide range of movement and include the shoulder, hip and knee.

4.5 The Muscular System

  • Skeletal muscle: Generally under voluntary control and moves the skeleton.
  • Cardiac muscle: Involuntary muscle found in the heart wall.
  • Smooth muscle: Involuntary muscle found in structures such as the intestines and blood vessels.

Chapter 5: The Cardiovascular System

5.1 Main Components

The cardiovascular system consists of the heart, blood and blood vessels. Its major functions include transporting oxygen, nutrients, hormones and waste products.

5.2 Chambers of the Heart

  • Right atrium: Receives oxygen-poor blood returning from the body.
  • Right ventricle: Pumps blood toward the lungs through the pulmonary arteries.
  • Left atrium: Receives oxygen-rich blood from the lungs through the pulmonary veins.
  • Left ventricle: Pumps blood into the aorta for distribution around the body.

5.3 Types of Blood Vessels

Arteries carry blood away from the heart. Veins return blood toward the heart. Capillaries are tiny vessels where exchange of gases, nutrients and wastes occurs.

5.4 Blood Components

  • Plasma: The liquid component carrying proteins, nutrients, hormones and waste products.
  • Red blood cells: Contain haemoglobin, which transports oxygen.
  • White blood cells: Participate in immune defence.
  • Platelets: Help form blood clots and control bleeding.
MLS connection: Haematology studies blood and blood-forming tissues. Common laboratory investigations include a full blood count, blood film examination and blood grouping.

Chapter 6: The Respiratory System

6.1 Main Organs

The respiratory system includes the nose, pharynx, larynx, trachea, bronchi and lungs. Its major function is gas exchange: oxygen enters the blood and carbon dioxide is removed.

6.2 Pathway of Air

  1. Nose or nasal cavity.
  2. Pharynx.
  3. Larynx.
  4. Trachea.
  5. Main bronchi.
  6. Smaller bronchi and bronchioles.
  7. Alveoli, where gas exchange occurs.

6.3 The Alveoli

Alveoli are tiny air sacs with thin walls and a rich capillary supply. Their structure allows oxygen and carbon dioxide to diffuse between air and blood.

6.4 The Diaphragm

The diaphragm is the main muscle of inspiration. When it contracts, it moves downward and increases the volume of the thoracic cavity. During quiet expiration, it relaxes and the thoracic cavity returns toward its resting volume.

Chapter 7: The Digestive System

7.1 Main Organs

The digestive tract includes the mouth, pharynx, oesophagus, stomach, small intestine, large intestine, rectum and anal canal. Accessory organs include the salivary glands, liver, gallbladder and pancreas.

7.2 Functions of Important Organs

  • Mouth: Receives food and begins mechanical digestion.
  • Oesophagus: Moves food toward the stomach by peristalsis.
  • Stomach: Mixes food with gastric secretions and begins substantial protein digestion.
  • Small intestine: Performs much digestion and absorbs most nutrients.
  • Large intestine: Absorbs water and electrolytes and helps form faeces.
  • Liver: Processes nutrients, produces bile and performs many metabolic functions.
  • Gallbladder: Stores and concentrates bile.
  • Pancreas: Releases digestive enzymes and bicarbonate; also produces hormones such as insulin and glucagon.

7.3 Importance in Medical Laboratory Science

Knowledge of digestive anatomy supports the understanding of liver function tests, pancreatic enzymes, stool analysis and diseases affecting the gastrointestinal tract.

Chapter 8: The Urinary System

8.1 Main Organs

The urinary system consists of two kidneys, two ureters, the urinary bladder and the urethra.

8.2 Functions of the Kidneys

  • Filter blood and remove metabolic waste.
  • Regulate water and electrolyte balance.
  • Help maintain acid-base balance.
  • Contribute to blood pressure regulation.
  • Produce erythropoietin, which supports red blood cell production.
  • Activate vitamin D to help regulate calcium balance.

8.3 The Nephron

The nephron is the functional unit of the kidney. Its main parts include the renal corpuscle, proximal convoluted tubule, loop of Henle, distal convoluted tubule and collecting system.

Urine formation involves three major processes:

  1. Filtration: Water and small solutes pass from blood into the renal tubule.
  2. Reabsorption: Useful substances move from the tubule back into the blood.
  3. Secretion: Certain substances move from the blood into the tubular fluid.
MLS connection: Urinalysis can help assess kidney and urinary tract health. Tests may examine colour, concentration, pH, protein, glucose, blood and microscopic sediment.

Chapter 9: The Nervous and Endocrine Systems

9.1 Nervous System

The nervous system receives information, processes it and coordinates responses. It consists of the central nervous system and peripheral nervous system.

  • Central nervous system: Brain and spinal cord.
  • Peripheral nervous system: Nerves and associated structures outside the brain and spinal cord.
  • Somatic division: Involved in sensation and control of skeletal muscles.
  • Autonomic division: Regulates functions such as heart rate, digestion and gland activity.

9.2 Endocrine System

The endocrine system consists of hormone-producing glands and tissues. Hormones travel through body fluids, including blood, to regulate processes such as growth, metabolism, reproduction and blood glucose.

Chapter 10: Body Cavities and Abdominal Regions

10.1 Major Body Cavities

  • Cranial cavity: Contains the brain.
  • Vertebral cavity: Contains the spinal cord.
  • Thoracic cavity: Contains the lungs and mediastinal structures, including the heart.
  • Abdominal cavity: Contains organs such as the stomach, liver, spleen, intestines and kidneys in the posterior abdominal region.
  • Pelvic cavity: Contains the urinary bladder, rectum and internal reproductive structures.

10.2 The Thorax

The thorax is protected by the ribs, sternum and thoracic vertebrae. The diaphragm separates the thoracic cavity from the abdominal cavity. The mediastinum is the central compartment between the lungs and contains the heart, major vessels, trachea and oesophagus, among other structures.

10.3 Four Quadrants of the Abdomen

  • Right upper quadrant (RUQ): Includes much of the liver and gallbladder.
  • Left upper quadrant (LUQ): Includes the stomach and spleen.
  • Right lower quadrant (RLQ): Includes the caecum and appendix.
  • Left lower quadrant (LLQ): Includes portions of the descending and sigmoid colon.

These are general descriptions; organs may extend beyond a single quadrant.
