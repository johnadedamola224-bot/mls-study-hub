BCH 201: General Biochemistry I

Topic 3: Classification and Chemistry of Proteins

MLS Study Hub | 200 Level

1. Learning Objectives

At the end of this lesson, students should be able to:

  • Define proteins and describe their general chemical composition.
  • Identify the major elements found in proteins.
  • Classify proteins according to chemical composition, shape and function.
  • Differentiate simple, conjugated and derived proteins.
  • Explain the relationship between amino acid sequence and protein structure.

2. Introduction to Proteins

Proteins are large biological molecules made from amino acid residues joined by peptide bonds. They are essential components of cells and tissues and perform many functions, including catalysis, transport, defence, movement and structural support.

Proteins contain carbon (C), hydrogen (H), oxygen (O) and nitrogen (N). Many contain sulfur (S), particularly when cysteine or methionine is present. Some proteins also contain phosphorus, metals or other elements through their associated groups.

The amino acid sequence of a protein influences how its chain folds, which in turn affects its structure and biological activity.

Key point: Proteins are polymers of amino acid residues. Their properties depend on amino acid composition, sequence, folding and any associated non-protein groups.

3. General Chemical Composition

Each amino acid residue in a protein contributes a carbon skeleton, amino-derived nitrogen and a side chain. The side chain varies between amino acids and helps determine the protein's chemical properties.

Element or componentImportance
CarbonForms the backbone and side-chain framework of amino acids.
HydrogenForms part of amino, carboxyl and side-chain groups.
OxygenOccurs in carbonyl groups and other oxygen-containing groups.
NitrogenOccurs in peptide bonds and amino acid side chains.
SulfurOccurs in cysteine and methionine; cysteine can form disulfide bonds.
Other componentsSome proteins associate with heme, carbohydrates, lipids, phosphate groups or metal ions.

Protein composition varies. No single elemental percentage applies to every protein.

4. Classification According to Chemical Composition

A. Simple proteins

Simple proteins yield mainly amino acids when completely hydrolysed, without a characteristic non-protein prosthetic group as part of their classification.

ExampleDescription
AlbuminsGenerally water-soluble proteins; serum albumin helps maintain oncotic pressure and transports substances.
GlobulinsA diverse group, many of which are less soluble in pure water than albumins. Immunoglobulins are antibodies.
HistonesBasic proteins that associate with DNA in chromatin.
ProlaminsPlant storage proteins, such as gliadin in wheat.
GlutelinsPlant storage proteins, including glutenin in wheat.
ScleroproteinsStructural proteins such as collagen and keratin.

These are traditional biochemical categories. Their solubility and properties depend on the particular protein and experimental conditions.

B. Conjugated proteins

Conjugated proteins contain a protein component together with a non-protein component called a prosthetic group or associated group.

C. Derived proteins

Derived proteins are products formed when native proteins undergo partial breakdown or chemical or physical modification.

  • Primary derived proteins: Traditional classifications include products formed through limited alteration, such as coagulated proteins.
  • Secondary derived proteins: Products of further breakdown, including proteoses, peptones and peptides in traditional schemes.

The exact terminology may vary among textbooks. Proteoses and peptones are historical biochemical terms for mixtures of protein digestion products.

5. Classification According to Shape

Fibrous proteins

Fibrous proteins have elongated structures and often provide strength or support. Many have limited solubility in water.

  • Collagen: Provides tensile strength in connective tissues.
  • Keratin: Contributes to the structure of hair, nails and the outer layer of skin.
  • Elastin: Helps tissues stretch and return towards their original shape.

Globular proteins

Globular proteins generally fold into compact shapes. Many are soluble in aqueous environments and perform dynamic functions.

  • Haemoglobin: Transports oxygen in red blood cells.
  • Albumin: Transports many substances in blood plasma.
  • Many enzymes: Catalyse biochemical reactions.

Membrane proteins

Membrane proteins associate with cell membranes. Some span the membrane and act as channels, transporters or receptors; others attach to one surface.

7. Levels of Protein Structure

Protein structure is commonly discussed at four levels. The detailed treatment of these levels belongs to the next structural topic, but the basic idea is introduced here.

LevelMeaningMain features
PrimaryAmino acid sequenceOrder of residues joined by peptide bonds
SecondaryLocal backbone foldingAlpha helices and beta sheets, stabilised mainly by backbone hydrogen bonds
TertiaryOverall three-dimensional structure of one polypeptideSide-chain interactions, hydrophobic effects, hydrogen bonds, ionic interactions and sometimes disulfide bonds
QuaternaryArrangement of multiple polypeptide subunitsPresent in proteins made from more than one subunit, such as haemoglobin

Not every protein has quaternary structure: a protein made from a single polypeptide chain does not require multiple subunits.

8. Factors Affecting Protein Properties

  • Amino acid composition: Side chains influence charge, polarity and solubility.
  • pH: Changes in pH can alter ionisation and protein interactions.
  • Temperature: High temperatures may disrupt the interactions that maintain folding.
  • Salt concentration: Salts can affect protein solubility and electrostatic interactions.
  • Solvents and chemicals: Certain solvents, detergents and denaturing agents alter protein structure.
  • Sequence and folding: The amino acid sequence strongly influences the protein's final structure and activity.

9. Denaturation of Proteins

Denaturation is the disruption of a protein's native structure without necessarily breaking its peptide-bond backbone. It often results in reduced or lost biological activity.

Common causes

  • High temperature
  • Extreme acidic or alkaline conditions
  • Organic solvents and detergents
  • Some heavy metal ions and chemical denaturants

Denaturation commonly affects secondary, tertiary or quaternary structure. The primary amino acid sequence usually remains intact unless chemical degradation also occurs.

Example: Heating egg white causes its proteins to unfold and aggregate, producing a change in appearance and texture.

10. Clinical and Laboratory Importance

  • Measurement of blood proteins can help assess nutritional status and certain diseases.
  • Serum protein electrophoresis separates proteins according to their movement in an electric field under specified conditions.
  • Enzyme assays measure the activity of particular proteins.
  • Protein purification is important in research, diagnosis and biotechnology.
  • Protein structure helps explain normal biological activity and the effects of certain genetic changes.

Laboratory results must always be interpreted with the relevant clinical information and the method's reference intervals.

12. Answers to Revision Questions

  1. A protein is a biological macromolecule composed of one or more polypeptide chains of amino acid residues.
  2. Carbon, hydrogen, oxygen and nitrogen. Many proteins also contain sulfur.
  3. Simple proteins yield mainly amino acids on complete hydrolysis; conjugated proteins contain a protein component and a non-protein component.
  4. Examples include glycoproteins, lipoproteins, phosphoproteins and metalloproteins.
  5. Derived proteins are products of the modification or partial breakdown of native proteins.
  6. Fibrous proteins are generally elongated and often structural; globular proteins are generally compact and often perform dynamic functions.
  7. Amylase; collagen; haemoglobin; immunoglobulin, respectively.
  8. Primary, secondary, tertiary and quaternary.
  9. Denaturation is the disruption of a protein's native structure, often with loss of function, without necessarily breaking its peptide backbone.
  10. Temperature, pH, salt concentration and denaturing chemicals.
  11. Examples include serum protein electrophoresis and measurement of enzyme activity.
  12. The sequence influences folding and the arrangement of chemical groups that enable a protein to perform its function.

13. Summary

  • Proteins are made of amino acid residues linked by peptide bonds.
  • They can be classified by chemical composition, shape and function.
  • Simple, conjugated and derived proteins are traditional composition-based categories.
  • Fibrous, globular and membrane proteins have different structural features.
  • Protein structure and chemical environment influence biological activity.
  • Denaturation can alter protein structure and reduce function.
