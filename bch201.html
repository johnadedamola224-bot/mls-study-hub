1. Introduction to BCH 201

Meaning of Biochemistry

Biochemistry is the branch of science concerned with the chemical substances found in living organisms and the reactions through which these substances are formed, modified, broken down and used. It explains the molecular basis of life.

Medical Laboratory Science students study biochemistry because many laboratory investigations measure chemical substances in blood, urine and other body fluids. Examples include glucose, urea, creatinine, cholesterol, proteins and selected enzymes. Results must be interpreted with appropriate clinical information.

Course contents

  • Introductory chemistry, classification, properties, reactions and functions of amino acids.
  • Peptides and the chemistry, classification and structures of proteins.
  • Protein and amino acid identification tests.
  • Isolation and purification of proteins.
  • Introductory chemistry of carbohydrates and lipids.
  • Nucleic acids, nucleosides, nucleotides and nucleic acid hydrolysis.
Learning objective: By the end of this course, students should be able to explain the chemistry of major biological molecules and relate their properties to their biological and laboratory significance.

2. Amino Acids: Introduction and Chemistry

2.1 Definition

Amino acids are organic compounds containing an amino group and a carboxyl group. The amino acids used to build proteins are mainly alpha-amino acids, in which the amino group and carboxyl group are attached to the same central carbon atom, called the alpha carbon.

The alpha carbon is also attached to a hydrogen atom and a variable side chain represented by the letter R. The identity of R gives each amino acid many of its distinctive chemical properties.

2.2 General structure of an alpha-amino acid

Diagram 1: The R group varies between amino acids. In solution, amino and carboxyl groups may be ionised.

2.3 The four groups attached to the alpha carbon

  1. Amino group: commonly written as –NH₂ in the un-ionised structural formula.
  2. Carboxyl group: written as –COOH in the un-ionised structural formula.
  3. Hydrogen atom: attached directly to the alpha carbon.
  4. R group: the variable side chain that influences the amino acid's size, charge, polarity and other properties.

2.4 Amino acids in aqueous solution

Amino acids can act as both acids and bases. In water, many amino acids exist predominantly as zwitterions at a suitable pH. A zwitterion has both a positive charge and a negative charge but may have an overall net charge of zero.

Diagram 2: The ionisation state depends on pH and the amino acid's ionisable groups.

At low pH, amino acids tend to accept protons and become more positively charged. At high pH, they tend to lose protons and become more negatively charged. The pH at which an amino acid has no overall net charge is called its isoelectric point, represented by pI.

2.5 Chirality and glycine

Most standard amino acids have a chiral alpha carbon because it is attached to four different groups. Glycine is the exception: its side chain is another hydrogen atom, so its alpha carbon is not chiral.

Proteins are made predominantly from L-amino acids. The L designation describes stereochemical configuration; it does not mean that an amino acid must rotate plane-polarised light to the left.

3. Classification of Amino Acids

3.1 Classification by side-chain properties

A useful method of classifying amino acids is by the chemical properties of their R groups. This helps predict their behaviour in water, their interactions with other molecules and their roles in protein structure.

GroupExamplesKey features
Non-polar Glycine, alanine, valine, leucine, isoleucine, methionine, proline Side chains are generally hydrophobic.
Polar, uncharged Serine, threonine, asparagine, glutamine Side chains can form hydrogen bonds.
Acidic Aspartate, glutamate Side chains usually carry negative charges near physiological pH.
Basic Lysine, arginine, histidine Side chains can accept protons; their charge depends on pH.
Aromatic Phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan Contain aromatic rings; tyrosine also has a polar hydroxyl group.

Note: These categories overlap. For example, aromatic amino acids can be classified separately while also having polar or non-polar properties.

3.2 Classification by charge

Acidic amino acids: Aspartic acid and glutamic acid contain an additional carboxyl group in the side chain. This group usually loses a proton near physiological pH, giving the side chain a negative charge.

Basic amino acids: Lysine, arginine and histidine contain side chains capable of accepting protons. Histidine is particularly important because its side-chain pKa is near the range where proton transfer can occur in many biological environments.

Neutral amino acids: In introductory classifications, this generally refers to amino acids without an additional acidic or basic side chain. They can still exist as charged zwitterions because their alpha-amino and alpha-carboxyl groups are ionisable.

3.3 Essential and nonessential amino acids

Essential amino acids must be obtained in adequate amounts from the diet because the body cannot synthesise them in sufficient quantities. Nonessential amino acids can generally be synthesised by the body. Some amino acids are conditionally essential during growth, illness or other circumstances.

CategoryExamples
Essential in humans Histidine, isoleucine, leucine, lysine, methionine, phenylalanine, threonine, tryptophan and valine
Commonly classified as nonessential Alanine, aspartate, asparagine, glutamate and serine
Conditionally essential in certain circumstances Examples can include arginine, cysteine, glutamine, glycine, proline and tyrosine, depending on age and physiological condition
Study tip: Memorise the nine essential amino acids and practise grouping amino acids by the properties of their side chains. The categories answer different questions, so do not confuse essentiality with polarity or charge.

4. Physical and Chemical Properties of Amino Acids

4.1 Physical properties

  • Solubility: Many amino acids dissolve in water, but their solubility varies with side-chain structure and pH.
  • Crystalline nature: Many amino acids form crystalline solids.
  • Melting behaviour: They often decompose at high temperatures rather than behaving like ordinary low-molecular-mass organic liquids.
  • Optical activity: Most standard amino acids are optically active because they are chiral; glycine is not.
  • Amphoteric behaviour: Amino acids can act as acids or bases.

4.2 Acid-base behaviour

The carboxyl group can donate a proton, while the amino group can accept a proton. The charge of an amino acid therefore changes with pH. Amino acids with ionisable side chains may have additional charged forms.

4.3 Reactions of amino acids

  1. Peptide bond formation: The carboxyl group of one amino acid can join to the amino group of another, forming a peptide bond in the resulting peptide.
  2. Reaction with acids and bases: Ionisable groups accept or donate protons according to pH.
  3. Decarboxylation: Removal of the carboxyl group as carbon dioxide can produce an amine; specific examples occur in biological pathways.
  4. Transamination: An amino group can be transferred between an amino acid and a keto acid in an enzyme-catalysed reaction.
  5. Disulphide bond formation: Two cysteine side chains can be oxidised to form a disulphide bond, helping stabilise some proteins.

1. Introduction to Biochemistry

Biochemistry is the branch of science that studies the chemical substances, reactions and processes occurring in living organisms. It combines principles of biology and chemistry to explain how cells obtain energy, build structures and maintain life.

Importance of biochemistry in Medical Laboratory Science

  • Helps explain normal body functions and metabolism.
  • Supports laboratory investigation of diseases.
  • Helps interpret substances measured in blood, urine and other samples.
  • Provides a foundation for clinical chemistry and related subjects.

2. Water, pH and Buffers

Water

Water is an important biological solvent. It helps dissolve many substances, transport materials, regulate temperature and support chemical reactions in the body.

pH

pH expresses how acidic or alkaline a solution is. A pH below 7 is acidic, pH 7 is neutral, and pH above 7 is alkaline at approximately 25°C.

Buffers

A buffer resists a large change in pH when small amounts of acid or base are added. Buffer systems help maintain a suitable pH for biological processes.

3. Carbohydrates

Carbohydrates are organic compounds made mainly of carbon, hydrogen and oxygen. They are important sources of energy and also contribute to cell structures and recognition.

Main classes

  • Monosaccharides: simple sugars such as glucose and fructose.

    5. Biological Functions of Amino Acids

    1. Protein synthesis: Amino acids are the building blocks of proteins.
    2. Metabolism: Their carbon skeletons can enter metabolic pathways and may contribute to energy production.
    3. Nitrogen transport: Certain amino acids help transport nitrogen between tissues.
    4. Formation of important compounds: Amino acids provide precursors for molecules such as neurotransmitters, hormones and other biologically active substances.
    5. Acid-base balance: Ionisable groups in amino acids and proteins can participate in buffering.
    6. Growth and tissue maintenance: An adequate supply is needed to synthesise and maintain body proteins.

    Medical Laboratory Science relevance

    Amino acid and protein chemistry helps students understand protein measurements, enzyme activity, metabolic disorders and specialised laboratory investigations. The significance of any laboratory result depends on the method used, specimen quality, reference interval and clinical context.

    6. Revision Questions

    Short-answer questions

    1. Define an amino acid and describe the general structure of an alpha-amino acid.
    2. What is a zwitterion?
    3. Explain the difference between essential and nonessential amino acids.
    4. List the nine essential amino acids in humans.
    5. Distinguish between acidic, basic, polar and non-polar amino acids.
    6. Why is glycine not optically active?
    7. Explain the meaning of the isoelectric point.
    8. Describe three important chemical reactions involving amino acids.
    9. Explain five biological functions of amino acids.
    10. What is a peptide bond?

    Multiple-choice questions

    1. Which part of an amino acid differs between most standard amino acids?

    A. Amino group
    B. Carboxyl group
    C. R group
    D. Alpha carbon

    2. Which standard amino acid is not chiral?

    A. Alanine
    B. Glycine
    C. Valine
    D. Leucine

    3. Which of the following is an essential amino acid in humans?

    A. Alanine
    B. Serine
    C. Leucine
    D. Aspartate

    4. Aspartate is commonly classified as:

    A. Acidic
    B. Basic
    C. Non-polar aromatic
    D. A carbohydrate

    5. The bond that joins amino acid residues in a protein is called a:

    A. Glycosidic bond
    B. Peptide bond
    C. Phosphodiester bond
    D. Hydrogen bond

    Answers

    1. C — R group.
    2. B — Glycine.
    3. C — Leucine.
    4. A — Acidic.
    5. B — Peptide bond.
